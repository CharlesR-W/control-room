# The Narrows

## Appointment briefing

- **To:** Minister for National Supply
- **From:** Emergency Economic Coordination Council
- **Date:** 4 September 1978
- **Classification:** Training scenario — fictional

Minister,

Cyclone Ione has damaged Selene’s only deep-water port. The country is not yet in collapse, but its margin for error is thin. Grain and diesel arrive by sea. Copper exports leave through the same port and earn the foreign exchange used to pay for those imports. Rail capacity links the port, regional depots, and the copper mine. Repairs compete for port space, diesel, money, and the same implementation teams needed for emergency measures.

Your mandate covers the next twelve weeks.

## Priorities

In order:

1. avoid severe regional food shortfalls;
2. preserve essential diesel services;
3. keep foreign-exchange reserves above the USD 10 million emergency floor;
4. restore useful port throughput;
5. limit hardship and contractual penalties; and
6. leave a defensible reserve position after the immediate crisis.

These priorities are not converted into a single public score. A decision may protect one objective while weakening another; your task is to understand and defend that trade.

## Known position

- National grain holdings total approximately 28 kt, split between central and regional stocks.
- Domestic grain output is estimated at 3 kt per week against gross demand of about 7 kt per week.
- Diesel holdings are 10 kt, with 1 kt per week of domestic supply.
- The port can currently process about 12 kt per week, down from 20 kt before the cyclone.
- Rail capacity is 10 kt per week. Grain and copper share it.
- Foreign-exchange reserves stand at USD 30 million.
- Six implementation teams are available across repair, audit, rationing, and emergency work.
- Some import cargo is already in the shipping pipeline.

Treat those figures as dated reports, not omniscient truth. Regional returns can contain persistent error; crop and engineering estimates may be revised. A targeted audit can reduce one uncertainty after a delay, but no information action reveals the whole model.

## How to operate

Each week:

1. inspect the situation, reports, incoming shipments, and binding constraints;
2. draft a package of procurement, allocation, operating, repair, information, and financing decisions;
3. record your forecast and reasoning;
4. resolve validation errors and review direct commitments;
5. commit the package and advance one week; and
6. inspect the events, observations, ledger effects, and causal trace before acting again.

An import contract does not put goods into a depot. It creates a shipment that must travel, arrive, unload through a constrained port, and move through a constrained inland network. Likewise, copper earns nothing until it reaches the port and is exported.

There is no requirement to change every policy every week. Churn consumes attention, and waiting can be a deliberate decision.

## First-week question

Before placing an order, estimate how many weeks of grain coverage remain after accounting for domestic output and demand. Then ask whether the supplier’s lead time leaves enough margin for shipping, port queues, and regional distribution.

The purpose of this exercise is not to discover a hidden perfect answer. It is to build a causal account of what is likely to bind next—and to update that account when reports or outcomes prove it wrong.
